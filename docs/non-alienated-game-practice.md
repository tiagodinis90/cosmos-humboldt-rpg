# Non-alienated game creation — creative practice

Status: working philosophy and creative roadmap, saved 2026-10-10. This is a cross-project practice archived here alongside COSMOS; it is **not** a binding feature specification for COSMOS. Original source: discussion and uploaded Gemini transcript, “Manifesto for Non-Commercial Game Creation”; the provocative opening manifesto was quoted there, and the theoretical expansion was produced in subsequent conversation. Do not attribute the whole framework to Marx, Debord or the unidentified manifesto author.

## Purpose
Make games because making games is worth doing. Economic success, wishlists, followers, reviews, hours played and commercial viability must not decide what is worth making. The phrase “no one will buy your videogame” is a *thought experiment*, not a sales forecast or prohibition on earning money.

## Theoretical anchors — interpretations, not settled proofs
- **Marx:** alienation from product, activity, creative potential and fellow creators; use value versus exchange value. Free distribution does not by itself eliminate alienated labour.
- **Mark Fisher:** capitalist realism and the assumption every hobby must become a side hustle. Ask why a game needs a market before assuming it does.
- **Situationists / Debord:** dérive (wandering without functional objectives); détournement (turning familiar interfaces against their usual meanings); constructed situations (brief experiences that interrupt routine). These are artistic techniques, not a licence to abuse players.
- **Michel de Certeau:** everyday tactics and appropriation; allow players to use systems unexpectedly.
- **Paul Lafargue:** leisure and the critique of productivity as a moral obligation.
- **Critical game studies:** distinguish claims made by procedural systems from their creators' declared politics. Bogost, Juul and Flanagan are suggested starting points; primary texts and interpretations need source checking.

## Principles
1. Begin with a question, encounter, emotion, systems experiment or joke, not a sales pitch.
2. Keep a separate place for tiny experiments and long-form work. A serious game can be polished, large and beautiful.
3. Prefer open, accessible and portable tools when they reduce barriers, not as an absolute purity test.
4. Preserve failed work and document discoveries, but never let archiving become compulsory busywork.
5. Break conventions with intent; preserve accessibility, player trust, consent and data integrity. **Never actually erase a player's unrelated files or misrepresent destructive behaviour as safe.**
6. The audience is made of people, not retention metrics. It is fine to make work that is difficult or uncomfortable; it is not fine to justify arbitrary exclusion as politics.
7. Non-commercial voluntary art must not excuse unpaid professional labour, precarity, or uncompensated collaborators. Credit and compensate when possible.
8. Free/open distribution, collaborative practice and mutual aid can build a commons, though platforms and licences have limitations.
9. Do not transform anti-productivity into a quota cult: schedules are supports, not obligations.
10. A market release remains a choice, not a betrayal or the ultimate destination.

## Modes of making
- **Microgames:** 15–90 minutes; one idea or interaction; export when possible.
- **Experiments:** 1–4 weeks; test a mechanic, situation or narrative structure.
- **Long game:** months/years; COSMOS is one possible deep track, with independent technical and artistic standards.

## Design territories
- Inner dialogue as competing interpretations rather than optimised RPG builds.
- Missing, unreliable and self-changing archives; avoid harming real player data.
- Psychogeographic drifts through Almada/Lisbon and fictional cities.
- Bureaucracy, forms, procurement, policy, permits and game interfaces as détournement.
- Environmental and infrastructure systems: energy, pollution, water, materials, waste and externalities.
- Ecology, memory, history and the political distribution of knowledge.

## Tools
Twine for hypertext, Ink for stateful dialogue, Decker for multimedia/card UI, Bitsy for tiny worlds, Godot for larger systemic experiments; Unity remains optional. Select the smallest tool that supports the idea.

## One-session protocol (optional, not a performance metric)
0–10 min: question and constraint. 10–40: prototype. 40–50: surprising intervention or revision. 50–60: export, preserve, and share if desired. Safety, accessibility and honest playtest feedback take precedence over artificial "no fixes" rules.

## Working rhythm (optional)
Read/observe; make a small thing; work on a deep project; play other people's strange games; jot down one discovery. No prescribed weekly output is required.

## Distribution and archive
Free browser builds on itch.io are one option. Store source, builds, dates, initial intention, what changed, what failed, what was discovered, rights and collaborator credits. Choose licence deliberately rather than assuming "free" means open source. Do not rehost the source manifesto without checking rights.

## Questions for each experiment
Would this be worth making without sales or praise? What convention am I examining? What did I learn that I could not predict? Could the work be smaller or stranger? Does the experiment respect the players and collaborators involved? What can I share?

## Important qualification
The manifesto's rhetorical claims that all polish, usability, tutorials or commercial production are capitalist impositions are **positions to interrogate**, not established findings. Accessibility and quality can be forms of care. Art's economic conditions and creators' material needs are real. Protect creative autonomy without romanticising poverty.
